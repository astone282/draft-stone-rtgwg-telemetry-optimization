Telemetry-Driven Congestion Optimization

Abstract

This document describes Telemetry-Driven Congestion Optimization (TDCO), a solution in which a centralized controller uses near-real-time per-tunnel demand and per-interface traffic measurements to prevent and alleviate link overload autonomously. The controller changes the paths of the tunnels under its control through existing mechanisms such as the Path Computation Element Communication Protocol (PCEP), BGP Segment Routing (SR) Policy, or NETCONF/YANG. TDCO avoids placing Traffic Engineering (TE) tunnels where they would cause overload and, when overload occurs, re-routes only as many TE tunnels as are needed to relieve the overload onto paths with spare capacity that meet their full TE constraints.¶

This document describes the solution and the safety properties needed to operate it without operator approval of individual actions. It defines no protocol extensions.¶

1. Introduction

Network links can become overloaded when traffic grows unexpectedly, when link capacity is degraded, or when traffic is re-routed after a failure.¶

An overloaded link degrades the traffic that it carries. Negative effects may begin before a link is completely full, because short traffic bursts can exceed the link capacity even when the average load over a measurement interval is below it.¶

Alleviating an overload generally requires moving only some of the traffic on the link, onto paths that have spare capacity and that still meet the Traffic Engineering (TE) requirements of the traffic that is moved. A router upstream of an overloaded link can steer traffic quickly, but it does not in general know the load on the links of the alternate path or the TE constraints of the traffic that it steers. Network-wide re-optimization and capacity planning take both into account, but they operate on longer timescales and often move many TE tunnels at once from a snapshot. A centralized controller that holds a network-wide view of the topology, the TE tunnel paths, and the measured traffic is in a position to close these gaps.¶

This document describes Telemetry-Driven Congestion Optimization (TDCO), in which a centralized controller prevents and alleviates link overload by steering the TE tunnels whose paths it controls and that operator policy includes in optimization. The controller changes those paths through existing mechanisms, for example the Path Computation Element Communication Protocol (PCEP), BGP Segment Routing (SR) Policy, or NETCONF/YANG. The controller combines the TE topology and link capacities, the measured demand of each TE tunnel, and the measured traffic on each interface into a view of the load on every link, and uses that view in two ways:¶

Proactive component:

When the controller computes a path for a TE tunnel whose demand is known, it prefers paths on which that demand does not push any link past the trigger threshold. Traffic is therefore kept off links that are about to become overloaded, not only removed from links that already are.¶

Reactive component:

When the measured load on one or more links exceeds the trigger threshold, the controller re-routes only as many TE tunnels as are needed to bring each of those links back to the target threshold, preferring paths that do not fill any other link past the target threshold. Subsequent telemetry confirms the effect and drives any further adjustment, forming a closed loop.¶

A TE tunnel that was moved off a link returns to it only if the link then stays at or below the target threshold. This keeps headroom on the link and prevents TE tunnels from being moved back and forth.¶

TDCO has three defining properties:¶

No human in the loop:

The controller detects the overload, selects the TE tunnels to move, computes alternate paths, and re-routes the selected TE tunnels, all without operator approval of individual actions. Instead, safe operation relies on automated safety controls, such as requiring an overload to be sustained before acting, suspending optimization when telemetry is stale, and damping re-routes, as described in Section 9.3.¶

Measured demand:

TE tunnel demand is learned from near-real-time streaming telemetry, flow collection, or PCEP performance metrics, rather than from signalled reservations or planning data.¶

Full TE-based computation:

Paths are computed with each TE tunnel's complete TE constraint set and with bandwidth awareness, so that offloaded traffic does not overload other links or violate TE policies or Service Level Agreements (SLAs). Adjustments are made by changing the paths of TE tunnels at their headends, and are incremental.¶

The purpose of this document is to describe this solution, and the safety properties needed to run it without operator approval of individual actions, so that it can be deployed with a common understanding of its behavior.¶

TDCO applies equally to RSVP-TE signalled LSPs and to SR Policies on the SR-MPLS and SRv6 data planes, and accounts for both in a single view of link load. This document does not define new protocol mechanisms. The algorithms used to select and place TE tunnels are left to implementations.¶

1.1. Relationship to Existing Work

TDCO builds on and is related to the following work:¶

  • [RFC9522] gives an overview of traffic engineering, including online, reactive congestion management. TDCO is an instance of that approach, carried out by a controller.¶

  • [RFC8051] describes use cases for stateful PCE, including optimization based on the traffic that LSPs carry. TDCO describes one such use case in detail, including the safety properties needed to run it autonomously, and applies it to any controller that can change TE tunnel paths, whether or not that controller is a PCE.¶

  • [RFC8426] describes models for the coexistence of RSVP-TE and SR LSPs. TDCO follows the centralized management of available capacity model (Section 3.2 of [RFC8426]).¶

2. Terminology

This document uses the terms Path Computation Element (PCE), Path Computation Client (PCC), and Label Switched Path (LSP) as defined in [RFC4655] and [RFC5440], and the stateful PCE terms delegation and PCE-initiated LSP as defined in [RFC8231] and [RFC8281].¶

Where this document uses the term LSP in a general sense, the concepts described apply equally to SR Policies on both the SR-MPLS and SRv6 data planes.¶

In addition, this document uses the following terms:¶

Controller:

A centralized entity that performs TDCO for the TE tunnels whose paths it controls. A stateful PCE [RFC8231] is one example of a controller.¶

TE Tunnel:

An SR Policy [RFC9256] or an RSVP-TE signalled LSP [RFC3209] whose path the controller is permitted to change, using one of the mechanisms described in Section 3.1. Examples include LSPs delegated to the controller [RFC8231], LSPs initiated by the controller [RFC8281], and SR Policy candidate paths that the controller originates.¶

TE tunnel demand:

The measured rate of traffic carried by a TE tunnel, as learned by the controller from streaming telemetry, flow collection, or PCEP performance metrics. TE tunnel demand is distinct from any bandwidth that is signalled or reserved for the TE tunnel.¶

Dark traffic:

Traffic carried on a link that is not carried in a TE tunnel, for example native IPv4, native IPv6, or MPLS traffic that follows shortest paths such as Label Distribution Protocol (LDP) [RFC5036], native SR, or Flexible Algorithm [RFC9350] traffic.¶

Link load:

The load on one direction of a link, as determined by the controller from the measured utilization learned through streaming telemetry or from the utilization advertised in the IGP or BGP-LS, adjusted for TE tunnel path changes that are in flight and for bandwidth reservations that the measurements do not reflect. Link load includes dark traffic (see Section 4.3). Link load is the value against which overload detection and placement decisions are checked.¶

Trigger threshold:

The configured link load, expressed as an absolute value or as a percentage of link capacity, above which a link is considered overloaded.¶

Target threshold:

The configured link load, less than or equal to the trigger threshold, to which the reactive component of TDCO aims to bring an overloaded link. It is also the highest load to which a link may be filled when moving TE tunnels that still have a valid path.¶

Link overload:

The condition in which the link load of a link exceeds its trigger threshold.¶

Mandatory placement:

A path computation for a TE tunnel that has no valid path and therefore must be placed, for example a new TE tunnel, a TE tunnel whose path has failed, or a TE tunnel whose path violates its constraints. All other path changes move a TE tunnel that already has a valid path; they are discretionary and are checked against the target threshold (see Section 7).¶

Least congested path:

Among the paths that satisfy the TE constraints of a TE tunnel, the path that the controller considers to be the least congested. It is used only when no path that satisfies those constraints keeps every link at or below the applicable threshold. How congestion is compared between paths, and how the optimization objective is used among equally congested paths, is left to the implementation.¶

Optimization policy:

The operator policy that determines which TE tunnels are included in TDCO and which are excluded from it (see Section 3.2).¶

3. The Controller

In TDCO, the controller is a centralized entity that computes and changes the paths of the TE tunnels under its control. The controller:¶

  • Maintains a network-wide view that combines the TE topology and link capacities, the state and paths of the TE tunnels, the bandwidth accounting for each link and the measured traffic.¶

  • Decides on its own initiative when a TE tunnel is to be placed or moved, and onto which path, using the proactive and reactive components.¶

  • Enacts those decisions by changing the paths of TE tunnels at their headends.¶

  • Observes the result and makes further adjustments as needed.¶

Because the network-wide state and the decisions are held in one place, the controller can coordinate the moves that it makes, account for TE tunnels whose path changes are still in flight, and apply the automated safety controls described in Section 9.3.¶

3.1. Enacting Path Changes

TDCO does not depend on a particular mechanism for changing the path of a TE tunnel. The controller needs a mechanism that lets it set the path of a TE tunnel at its headend, learn the current state and path of that TE tunnel, and learn when a path change is complete. Existing mechanisms include:¶

PCEP:

A stateful PCE [RFC8231] updates the paths of LSPs that are delegated to it, and can create LSPs itself [RFC8281]. Segment Routing paths are carried using the extensions for SR-MPLS [RFC8664] and SRv6 [RFC9603]. PCEP state reports tell the controller the current path and operational state of each LSP.¶

BGP SR Policy:

The controller originates SR Policy candidate paths and advertises them to headends in BGP [RFC9830]. This mechanism applies to SR Policies only. Changing the path of a TE tunnel means advertising an updated candidate path. BGP SR Policy does not itself report the state of the SR Policy at the headend. The controller learns that state by other means, for example from the BGP-LS advertisement of SR Policies [RFC9857].¶

NETCONF/YANG:

The controller configures TE tunnel paths on the headend through NETCONF [RFC6241], using YANG [RFC7950] data models for TE tunnels or SR Policies, and reads the operational state through the same data models.¶

A single controller can use different mechanisms for different TE tunnels or headends. The procedures in this document apply in the same way whichever mechanism is used. The mechanisms differ in how quickly a path change takes effect and in how the controller learns that it has taken effect. These differences affect the in-flight accounting described in Section 4.4.¶

When this document refers to a TE tunnel that is "controlled by" the controller, it means a TE tunnel whose path the controller is permitted to change through one of these mechanisms.¶

3.2. Optimization Policy

An optimization policy configured by the operator determines which controlled TE tunnels are included in TDCO and which are excluded from it. An excluded TE tunnel is not moved by the reactive component and is not moved by TDCO back toward its preferred path. It still receives a path when it needs a mandatory placement.¶

The demand of an excluded TE tunnel remains part of the link load, in the same way as the demand of TE tunnels that are not controlled by the controller, and is taken into account when other TE tunnels are placed or moved.¶

4. Inputs

4.1. Topology and Capacity

The controller learns the network topology, link capacities, and TE attributes through BGP-LS [RFC9552] or by participating in the IGP.¶

4.2. TE Tunnel Demand

The controller learns the actual traffic demand of each TE tunnel from near-real-time measurements. Possible sources include:¶

  • Streaming telemetry, for example gNMI over gRPC [GNMI].¶

  • Flow collection, for example IPFIX [RFC7011].¶

  • PCEP performance metrics sent by the headends [I-D.gandhi-pce-pm].¶

Demand is tracked per TE tunnel. Because the controller uses measured demand rather than signalled reservations or planning data, its view reflects the traffic that the network is actually carrying.¶

4.3. Link Load

The controller learns the load on each link from near-real-time measurements. Possible sources include:¶

  • Streaming telemetry of interface counters, for example gNMI over gRPC [GNMI].¶

  • The Unidirectional Utilized Bandwidth advertised in the IGP TE metric extensions [RFC7471] [RFC8570].¶

  • The Unidirectional Utilized Bandwidth carried in BGP-LS [RFC8571].¶

Each of these sources measures all traffic on the link, including dark traffic. Dark traffic is therefore accounted for in link load, even though it is not steered.¶

The sources differ in the detail they provide. Streaming telemetry can break the load down by traffic type, for example native IPv4, native IPv6, and MPLS traffic, although the breakdowns available differ between network device platforms. The IGP and BGP-LS advertise a single utilization value for each direction, and how current that value is depends on the advertisement interval and on any thresholds that govern re-advertisement. The sources can be used alone or together.¶

The controller combines the measured interface traffic and the measured TE tunnel demand to determine the link load. The exact formula is implementation-specific.¶

The link load is used for detecting overload and for placement decisions.¶

4.4. In-Flight Path Changes

Telemetry lags behind path changes. After a TE tunnel is moved, for example by make-before-break, it can take one or more measurement intervals before the measurements show its traffic leaving the old path and arriving on the new one.¶

This needs to be accounted for in both directions:¶

  • On the links of the old path, the TE tunnel's demand is treated as already removed. Otherwise an overloaded link continues to appear overloaded, and the controller moves more TE tunnels than are needed.¶

  • On the links of the new path, the TE tunnel's demand is treated as already added. Otherwise the capacity taken by the move still appears free, and the controller can give it to another TE tunnel.¶

The controller applies these adjustments until the move is complete and the measurements reflect it. How completion is determined, and how the adjustments are made, are implementation-specific.¶

Responsiveness is bounded by the telemetry or collection interval and by any holding timers that apply before a threshold crossing is acted upon.¶

5. Proactive Component: Avoiding Busy Links

The controller needs to perform a mandatory placement for numerous reasons, including, but not limited to, the creation of a new TE tunnel, a link failure, or a constraint violation.¶

When the controller performs a mandatory placement for a TE tunnel whose demand is known, it prefers any path on which the TE tunnel's demand does not push the link load of any link past the trigger threshold. This is a preference rather than a constraint: if no path avoids the trigger threshold, the TE tunnel is placed on the least congested path that satisfies its other TE constraints, rather than being rejected, and any resulting overload is handled by the reactive component. Traffic is therefore steered away from links that would become overloaded, not only removed from links that already are.¶

A TE tunnel that was moved off a link by the reactive component may revert to its preferred path through normal re-optimization, only when every link of that path stays at or below the target threshold after the TE tunnel's demand is added.¶

6. Reactive Component: Overload Alleviation

When the measured load on a link exceeds the trigger threshold, and this is sustained as described in Section 9.3, the controller calculates the excess of the link load above the target threshold. It then selects TE tunnels, from those that the optimization policy includes in TDCO (see Section 3.2), whose combined demand on the link covers the excess, and re-routes only those TE tunnels. If a selected TE tunnel cannot be placed on an alternate path that satisfies its constraints and that does not fill any link past the target threshold, it is placed on the least congested path that satisfies its constraints, as described in Section 7, and the controller can select other TE tunnels to cover the remaining excess.¶

One or many links can be overloaded at the same time, for example when a failure shifts traffic onto several links. The controller takes all of the overloaded links into consideration together when selecting the TE tunnels to move, rather than relieving each link in isolation.¶

The controller aims to move no more TE tunnels than are needed, because each move adds signalling load and churn and carries a risk of traffic disruption. Choosing the smallest set of TE tunnels that covers the excess on every overloaded link, given that each TE tunnel needs a feasible alternate path, is a combinatorial problem, and the fewest TE tunnels are not always the best choice: the largest TE tunnels are the hardest to place and the most disruptive to move. The choice of which TE tunnels are best suited to be moved is therefore left to the implementation.¶

Each overloaded link is continuously re-evaluated with new telemetry as traffic moves, to ensure that the target threshold is met. During re-evaluation, the controller takes into account TE tunnels whose path changes are still in flight, so that it does not move more TE tunnels than are needed.¶

The overload of a link can be unresolvable, for example because it is caused mainly by dark traffic, by TE tunnels that are not controlled by the controller, or by TE tunnels that the optimization policy excludes from TDCO, because no movable TE tunnel has a feasible alternate path, or because the demand of a single TE tunnel exceeds the headroom of every feasible alternate path. TE tunnels are moved as a whole and are not split across paths.¶

7. Path Computation

Alternate paths are computed with each TE tunnel's full TE constraint set, for example its metric bounds, affinities, diversity requirements, and bidirectional co-routing constraints, and with its optimization objective, for example lowest latency. Among the paths that satisfy the constraints and keep every link at or below the applicable threshold, the controller selects the path that best meets the optimization objective. The least congested path is used only when no such path exists.¶

Path computation is bandwidth-aware: it uses the measured demand of the TE tunnel being placed or moved and the link load of candidate links, checked against the trigger and target thresholds. As a result, traffic moved off one link is kept off links that it would overload whenever another path allows it, and the TE tunnel's TE requirements continue to be met.¶

The trigger threshold is used to detect an overloaded link and to choose a path for a mandatory placement. A mandatory placement avoids pushing any link past the trigger threshold when it can. When it cannot, the TE tunnel is still placed, on the least congested path, and any resulting overload is handled by the reactive component.¶

Every other path change, whether made by the reactive component, by a TE tunnel reverting to its preferred path, or by re-optimization, uses the target threshold. The TE tunnel is moved onto a path on which every link stays at or below the target threshold after its demand is added. If no such path exists, the TE tunnel stays on its current path as long as every link of that path is at or below the trigger threshold. Otherwise, it is moved onto the least congested path that satisfies its other TE constraints, and any resulting overload is handled by the reactive component. When the target is set below the trigger, the gap between them leaves headroom, so small increases in load do not cause a new overload and TE tunnels are not moved back and forth.¶

The adjustment is local and incremental. Only the TE tunnels required to relieve the overloaded link are moved. The reactive component does not perform network-wide re-optimization. Periodic or operator-requested re-optimization is a separate process, and the moves that it makes are checked against the target threshold.¶

8. Bandwidth Management

The controller manages bandwidth for both RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels and SR Policies. It maintains the bandwidth accounting for each link itself, rather than relying on the network. SR Policies have no per-hop reservation state in the network, so the controller is the only place where their bandwidth is accounted for. Because the same accounting covers both kinds of TE tunnel, RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels and SR Policies can share links, with the placement of each taking into account the bandwidth used by the other.¶

TDCO does not perform bandwidth admission control: bandwidth determines where a TE tunnel is placed, not whether it is placed. A TE tunnel is never rejected or torn down for lack of bandwidth. When no path that satisfies the TE constraints of the TE tunnel has enough headroom for its demand, the TE tunnel is placed on the least congested of those paths, for example the path that minimizes the highest link load along it, and any resulting overload is handled by the reactive component. This applies equally to RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels and SR Policies.¶

This is the centralized management of available capacity model described in Section 3.2 of [RFC8426].¶

The controller keeps a single link load for each link, which combines the measurement and signalled inputs described in Section 4.3 and Section 8.1.¶

8.1. RSVP-TE Bandwidth Signalling

RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels may or may not signal a bandwidth reservation. It is generally assumed that RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels that are controlled by the controller do not signal a bandwidth reservation for admission, and that the controller computes and manages their bandwidth itself, using telemetry. A signalled reservation causes each node along the path to perform its own admission against the signalled value. That value can differ from the measured demand that the controller uses. Unlike the bandwidth checks made by the controller, which only influence path selection, admission at the nodes is a hard check: it can cause a path computed by the controller to be rejected, or can cause other LSPs to be preempted.¶

When RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels do signal a bandwidth reservation, the controller can account for the reservation by considering the maximum of the signalled bandwidth and the bandwidth measured by telemetry as the TE tunnel demand.¶

The controller also accounts for bandwidth reserved by RSVP-TE LSPs that it does not control. Such LSPs may still be reported to the controller, or may be unknown to it. When an LSP that the controller does not control is known to it, for example through PCEP state reports or NETCONF/YANG operational state from the headend, the controller learns its reservation and path, and can measure its demand using the same telemetry sources as for controlled TE tunnels. When an LSP is not known to the controller, the controller learns the bandwidth reserved on each link from the Unreserved Bandwidth advertised in the IGP TE extensions for OSPF (Section 2.5.8 of [RFC3630]) and IS-IS (Section 3.6 of [RFC5305]), or carried as a link attribute in BGP-LS [RFC9552]. In both cases the reservation is taken into consideration for the link load.¶

9. Operational Considerations

TDCO acts on measured traffic without operator approval of individual actions. Its safe operation therefore depends on the network it is deployed in, on acting only on measurements that are current, sustained, and reflect the effect of earlier actions, and on bounding how much it changes the network. This section describes where TDCO is applicable, what an operator needs to configure, the safety controls that an implementation should provide, and how operators retain control and visibility. The specific knobs and controls are implementation-specific.¶

9.1. Applicability

TDCO is applicable to networks in which:¶

  • The TE tunnels that carry a significant share of the traffic on the links to be protected are controlled by the controller and included in TDCO by the optimization policy. TDCO can only relieve overload by moving traffic that it controls and is permitted by policy to move.¶

  • The demand of each controlled TE tunnel can be measured in near real time, and the load of each link can be measured.¶

  • Headends can change the path of a TE tunnel without disrupting its traffic, for example by make-before-break for RSVP-TE signalled TE tunnels or by a candidate path update for SR Policies.¶

  • The topology offers alternate paths that satisfy the TE constraints of the TE tunnels to be moved.¶

  • A single controller is responsible for the TE tunnels whose paths share a set of links. Independent controllers that steer TE tunnels across the same links without coordination can react to the same overload and move more traffic than is needed.¶

TDCO addresses sustained overload, measured over one or more collection intervals. It does not react to individual traffic bursts shorter than the collection interval. It also cannot relieve an overload that is caused mainly by dark traffic, by TE tunnels that are not controlled by the controller, or by TE tunnels that the optimization policy excludes from TDCO.¶

9.2. Configuration

An operator deploying TDCO needs to choose:¶

Collection interval:

The telemetry or flow-collection interval bounds how quickly an overload is detected. Shorter intervals improve responsiveness at the cost of additional collection load on the network devices, the collectors, and the controller.¶

Thresholds:

The trigger and target thresholds, configured globally or per link. The trigger threshold is typically set below link capacity, so that steering begins before traffic bursts cause loss. The gap between the trigger and target thresholds determines how far below the overload point a link is brought, and therefore how much headroom remains before the link triggers again.¶

Holding time:

The time, or number of consecutive measurement intervals, for which a threshold crossing must be sustained before it is acted upon. A longer holding time reduces sensitivity to transient load at the cost of slower response.¶

Optimization policy:

Which TE tunnels are included in TDCO and which are excluded, as described in Section 3.2. Excluding TE tunnels reduces the traffic that TDCO can move, and therefore the overloads that it can relieve.¶

9.3. Safety Controls

The number of TE tunnels moved for a single overload event is determined by the reactive component. The controls below are independent of that per-event selection and limit TDCO's overall behavior, including when its inputs are wrong. An implementation should consider:¶

Sustained threshold crossing:

A link is treated as overloaded only when its link load remains above the trigger threshold for the configured holding time. A single sample or a short traffic burst therefore does not cause TE tunnels to be moved.¶

Telemetry validity:

Optimization is suspended for the affected links or TE tunnels when telemetry is unavailable or stale, so that steering decisions are not made on data that no longer reflects the network. Where a TE tunnel has a signalled bandwidth reservation (see Section 8.1), the controller can use the signalled value as its demand while telemetry is unavailable. Suspension applies to discretionary path changes; mandatory placements are still performed, using the last known or signalled demand.¶

In-flight accounting:

Telemetry lags behind path changes. Until subsequent measurements reflect the TE tunnels that have already been moved, the controller accounts for those moves so that it does not react again to load that is already being removed.¶

Re-route damping:

The frequency of path re-routing, either per TE tunnel or globally, can be limited to reduce network-wide churn and potential high-frequency oscillation.¶

9.4. Operator Control and Visibility

Control:

Operators need to be able to disable TDCO, globally or for individual links, and to include TE tunnels in TDCO or exclude them from it through the optimization policy described in Section 3.2, for example to exclude TE tunnels that are pinned to an explicit path for service reasons.¶

Visibility:

Because individual actions are not approved by an operator, the controller needs to report the actions that it takes, including the overloaded link, its link load before and after the action, and the TE tunnels that were moved and their old and new paths. The controller also needs to report overloads that it cannot resolve, so that the operator can act on them, for example by adding capacity.¶

10. Security Considerations

This document uses existing protocols and does not introduce new protocol mechanisms. The security considerations of the mechanisms used to change TE tunnel paths apply: PCEP [RFC5440], stateful PCE [RFC8231], and PCE-initiated LSPs [RFC8281]; BGP SR Policy [RFC9830]; and NETCONF [RFC6241]. The security considerations of BGP-LS [RFC9552] and of the telemetry and flow-collection protocols in use also apply.¶

Because TDCO acts on measured data without operator approval of individual actions, the integrity of its inputs is particularly important:¶

  • Falsified or manipulated telemetry, flow records, or PCEP performance metrics could cause the controller to re-route TE tunnels unnecessarily, to fail to alleviate a genuine overload, or to steer traffic toward links chosen by an attacker. Telemetry and flow-collection sessions need to be authenticated and integrity protected, for example by running gNMI over gRPC with TLS, or by using the TLS or DTLS transports for IPFIX described in [RFC7011]. PCEP sessions can be protected using TLS [RFC8253]. The input plausibility control in Section 9.3 limits the effect of inputs that are falsified but well formed.¶

  • Telemetry collectors, and any processing pipeline between them and the controller, are on the controller's decision path. Compromise of a collector or of the pipeline has the same effect as falsified telemetry, even when each individual telemetry session is protected. These components need the same level of protection as the controller itself.¶

  • Compromise of the trigger and target threshold configuration could be used to induce steering or to suppress it. Protection of the configuration and management interfaces of the controller is therefore important.¶

  • An attacker able to inject bursty traffic might attempt to provoke repeated re-routing. The sustained threshold crossing and re-route damping controls in Section 9.3 limit the rate at which TDCO responds, and therefore limit the churn that such traffic can cause.¶

  • A controller already controls the paths of the TE tunnels under its control, whichever mechanism it uses to change them. TDCO does not change that, but autonomous operation increases the importance of protecting the controller itself and the sessions it uses to change paths, whether PCEP, BGP, or NETCONF.¶

Per-tunnel demand and flow records reveal the traffic matrix of the network and, potentially, the traffic volumes of individual customers. This information needs to be protected in transit, as described above, and at rest in the controller and in the collectors, with access restricted to authorized operators and systems.¶
